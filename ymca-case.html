The problem

The YMCA of Piscataway is an award-winning nonprofit community center with a broad membership menu. Leadership had little visibility into how its memberships and programs compared with other local options, and suspected its prices didn't reflect the value it offered. They asked us for two things: a benchmark against key competitors, and a revised pricing recommendation grounded in real market value.

Our approach

1. Map the current structure. We reviewed all 24 membership options (12 plan types, each with resident and non-resident rates) and found a menu that was more complex than members or staff needed.

2. Benchmark the market. We picked five competitors to cover the full range of the local market, from the lowest price a member could pay to the highest:

CompetitorRole in the analysis
Planet FitnessPrice floor (budget, no-frills)
24 Hour FitnessDirect gym competitor
LA FitnessMid-tier commercial gym
JCC of Middlesex CountyClosest peer (community center)
Life Time FitnessPrice ceiling (premium club)

For each one we compared membership types, pricing, fees and amenities against what the YMCA offers.

3. Model the revenue impact. I built the quantitative model. It estimated monthly membership revenue under the current structure and under our proposed structure, then stress-tested the result by assuming a meaningful share of members leave after prices change.

What we found

  • The YMCA was priced below its true competitive value. Its closest peer charged more for near-identical amenities.
  • Its strongest advantages were family and community value, including aquatics and programming that commercial gyms don't match.
  • Budget and mid-tier gyms compete hardest on individual adult pricing, so that is where the YMCA should price most carefully.

The recommendation

  • Consolidate the menu into a few clear categories (teen, adult, senior and family) so it is easier to understand and administer.
  • Use flat family pricing with a set rate for each additional member.
  • Apply one consistent rule for the resident / non-resident difference instead of setting each rate case by case.
  • Raise prices to reflect market value while staying competitive with local community centers and gyms.

The result

Even in a downside case where 20% of members leave over the year, the model projects monthly membership revenue about 17% higher than under the original structure. We presented the analysis and recommendations to YMCA leadership, along with next steps: measuring member price sensitivity, studying how other YMCAs handled price changes, and reviewing facility costs.

My role

I owned the quantitative analysis: structuring the model's assumptions, building the revenue comparison and presenting the financial results to the client. I also helped the team connect the competitive research to the final pricing structure.

This summary covers the approach and outcomes only. The client's membership data, internal pricing and the full deliverable are confidential to the YMCA of Piscataway and Rutgers 180 Degrees Consulting.
